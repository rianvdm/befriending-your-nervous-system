All explainers

Brainspotting, for beginners

Where you
look affects
how you feel

That is Brainspotting’s own slogan. The therapy behind it starts with one question: which way are your eyes pointing?

Scroll

01  /  The accident

It was found by mistake, in 2003.

A sixteen-year-old figure skater, a year of weekly ninety-minute sessions, and one jump she still could not land. Her therapist, a New York psychotherapist called David Grand, was running an EMDR session — eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing, where you follow the therapist’s moving finger with your eyes. Hers reached one point in the air, wobbled hard and froze.

So he stopped too, and held his finger where it was. For the next ten minutes she worked through material that a year of talking had not reached. She rang him from the rink the following morning: she had landed the triple loop, then landed it again and again.

“It was like a hand grabbed my wrist and held my finger in place.”

David Grand

02  /  Why eyes

Looking is a survival move.

Turning to look is among the oldest things an animal does. A sound, and the head swings round before anything has been decided. Vision is how you check a room, and Grand’s argument is that the checking runs through the midbrain, under the part of you that makes sentences.

Which would make the direction of your gaze live information rather than an accident of where the furniture is.

“Where we look at different times is not random — in fact, I believe it is never random.”

David Grand

03  /  The map

A brainspot is a place to look.

It is an eye position that lines up with whatever you are carrying. There is no chart to look it up on: up and to the left means one thing for you and something else entirely for whoever sits in that chair after you. It is only ever the place where, for you, today, the feeling gets loudest.

Yours, and only today’s.

Find the same spot four sessions running, Grand says, and the fifth will be somewhere else entirely.

“With brainspotting, each person has their own map.”

David Grand

04  /  Finding it

Three ways to land on the spot.

They arrive at the same place: your eyes fixed on one point, and held there. What differs is who does the finding. (Grand swapped his finger for a telescopic pointer early on, since nobody can hold an arm up that long.)

01 · found from the outside

Outside window

The therapist watches your face while the pointer travels, and stops wherever something flickers.

A blink, a swallow, a lick of the lips, a tilt of the head, eyes that wobble. Grand’s original method. Also the slowest.

02 · found from the inside

Inside window

The therapist asks where you feel it most, and you are the one who says.

Left, middle or right; then above eye level, at it, or below. Quicker, because you are reading your own map.

03 · already happening

Gazespotting

You found it yourself, five minutes ago, talking to a spot on the wall.

The last one Grand worked out and, he says, the one he should have seen first. Gentler than the other two.

05  /  A session

Then nothing.

The setup takes a few minutes. After that there is no technique to perform.

1

Name it

One thing to work on. A specific moment usually does more than a whole decade.

2

Rate it

How strong is it right now, zero to ten? Brainspotting calls this activation deliberately, because plenty of people feel something they would never call distress.

3

Locate it

Where is it in your body? Chest, throat, stomach, jaw. That answer anchors everything after it.

4

Find the spot

One of the three ways above. A pointer goes up and stays there.

5

Stay

Eyes on the spot. Watch whatever turns up and follow it. Speak if you want to; most people say very little.

“The brain knows what to do. And 99 percent of the time, it knows what to do better than the therapist does.”

David Grand

06  /  Below the words

You cannot explain your way down.

The claim underneath all of this is that the worst material sits below language, in the older parts of the brain, where there is no vocabulary and no story available. Conversation works on the part of you that has words. The rest stays where it is.

It is one way to read the skater: a year of good conversation, and the jump exactly where she had left it.

“At that point, there’s no language and there’s no story — it is just all felt sense and body experience.”

David Grand

07  /  The person opposite

The pointer is the smaller half.

Grand calls the working conditions a dual attunement frame: someone tuned to you, while you are tuned to a point in space. He puts the relationship first and tells his trainees so on day one, because the pointer is the thing in the room that looks like it is doing the work.

His picture for it is a comet.

“The client is the comet, and the therapist tries to get in the tail of the comet to follow wherever the comet goes.”

David Grand

08  /  The vocabulary

A small private language.

Brainspotting renamed a handful of things other therapies already had words for, and the choices tell you what it thinks it is doing.

ActivationWhat other approaches call distress. Kept broad on purpose, so it fits people who cannot name the thing they feel.
ProcessingWhatever arrives while you sit on the spot. Images, memories, sensation, or a long stretch of very little.
Focused mindfulnessWatching your inside with your outside held still on one point.
Resource spotAn eye position found by asking where you feel calmest in your body, for people the ordinary version overwhelms.
BioLateral soundGrand’s own recordings, panned slowly from ear to ear and played low under a session. He made the first one on cassette.

09  /  Catch yourself

You have been doing half of it for years.

The therapy needs a trained therapist and a good reason. The reflex it is built on costs nothing to observe.

Next time you are thinking hard, or telling someone something that matters, notice where your eyes go. Rarely at them. Some fixed point off to one side, held for minutes at a time. Grand spent years of his own analysis staring at a crack in the ceiling, and worked out what he had been doing roughly twenty-five years later.

“You just see that you were looking at a spot on the carpet, and when you come out of it after ten minutes or so, you say, ‘Why was I looking over there?’”

David Grand

10  /  The limits

He teaches the not knowing.

Grand takes a name from physics for this and calls it the uncertainty principle. His version: the brain across from you is too complicated to read, so the moment you are sure why something is working, you have stopped paying attention to the person it is happening to.

Few people who invent a method write that into its training.

“I am pretty convinced that there is a part of this that we are never going to fully understand.”

David Grand

The short version

Hold your eyes on one spot and stay there while your body works.

Grand’s bet is that the eye position matters, and that you do better with the therapist out of the way.

Brainspotting is thinly evidenced and openly disputed. The strongest study its own developers list is Glantschnig’s 2025 trial of a hundred private-practice clients, every one of them seen by the single therapist who ran the study, and her own conclusion is that randomised controlled trials are still needed. D’Antoni’s 2022 comparison found one session cut distress about as much as a session of EMDR did, but its forty participants were doctors and psychologists recalling upsetting memories, well short of a clinical trial. Against that, McKay and Coreil argued in 2024 that the proposed brain circuitry has no evidence behind it and that the method is built in a way that makes it hard to disprove; brainspotting researchers answered them in the same journal, and the argument is still running. The American Psychological Association’s 2025 guideline for treating PTSD in adults does not list brainspotting. This page sets out the method as Grand teaches it.
