01  /  The one question

All day, your body is asking one thing.

Am I safe? It answers long before you notice you asked.

This is the vagus nerve. Most of its traffic runs upward, from your organs to your brain. Your gut reports first. Your thinking arrives second.

“Eighty percent of the information is coming from your body and then being fed up to your brain.”

Deb Dana

02  /  Neuroception

It listens in three directions at once.

Not consciously. There is no decision to make. Stephen Porges named this listening neuroception: detection that happens underneath awareness.

Then it does the simplest arithmetic there is.

More safety
than danger?

You stay up.

More danger
than safety?

You go down.

03  /  The ladder

Down where?

Dana’s picture for this is a ladder. Three rungs. You are always standing on one of them.

Top rung · ventral vagal

Safe and social

“I’m OK. The world’s safe enough for me to move through.”

Warm. Curious. Able to listen, joke, plan, rest, be touched.

Newest
~200m years
mammals only

Middle rung · sympathetic

Fight or flight

“Something is wrong. I have to do something about it now.”

Restless, snappy, racing, braced. Anywhere from mild fidget to full panic.

Middle
~400m years

Bottom rung · dorsal vagal

Shut down

“It’s too much. There’s no point. I need to disappear.”

Foggy, flat, heavy, far away. The tortoise pulling into its shell.

Oldest
~500m years
we share it with fish

Notice the order they arrived in. The bottom rung is half a billion years old. The top rung is the newcomer — and the first thing you lose under pressure.

04  /  No skipping

You climb it a rung at a time.

There is no jump from shut down straight back to calm. The way out of the bottom runs through the middle. Collapse has to become movement before movement can become ease.

Which is why anger can be a sign of progress, if you have been numb for a while.

05  /  Story follows state

Your state picks the story.

This is Dana’s most useful sentence. The body lands somewhere first, and then the mind writes an explanation that fits. Same event, three different truths.

A friend hasn’t replied to your message.

From safe and social

“They’re busy. I’ll hear from them.”

From fight or flight

“What did I do? I need to fix this right now.”

From shut down

“Of course. Nobody really wants me around anyway.”

So arguing with the thought rarely works. The thought is a passenger. Move the state and the story changes on its own.

06  /  Why befriend it

Every rung has saved something.

Shutting down is not a failure of will. It is the oldest protection you own, doing exactly what it evolved to do when fighting and running were not options.

Dana’s word is befriending, and she means it literally. You get to know a friend. You stop demanding they be someone else. Then you keep at it.

“A friendship is not something you just do. You continue to nurture that friendship.”

Deb Dana

07  /  Co-regulation

You were never meant to do this alone.

A calm nervous system nearby calms yours. Dana calls this a biological imperative, not a nice extra. Babies cannot regulate alone, and adults never fully outgrow it.

Co-regulation comes first. Self-regulation is built on top of it. When someone had to learn to soothe themselves too early, self-soothing tends to cost them energy instead of giving it back.

“We need to feel safe — but not only safe. We need to feel safe in the arms of another.”

Deb Dana

08  /  Glimmers

The opposite of a trigger.

Dana coined the word. A trigger drops you down the ladder. A glimmer lifts you, half a second at a time. They are small enough to miss for an entire lifetime.

Sun through the kitchen window

A dog deciding it likes you

The first mouthful of coffee

Someone saying your name warmly

The opening bars of a song you forgot

Cold air on your face

Getting into a bed you made that morning

A stranger holding a door

Noticing them is the training. Each one is a rep.

09  /  The practice

Four moves, in order.

Dana teaches this as the four R’s. It is the whole method, compressed.

1

Recognise

Where am I on the ladder right now? Naming the rung is most of the work.

2

Respect

This state is trying to protect me. Fighting it adds a second problem on top of the first.

3

Regulate

Do the small physical thing that moves you up a rung. Or borrow someone else’s calm.

4

Re-story

Now, from higher up the ladder, ask what actually happened. The story will be different.

10  /  What actually moves you

Small, physical, unglamorous.

None of these are insights. They are things the body does, which is the point. And they are personal — what lifts one nervous system does nothing for the next.

A longer out-breathBreathe out for longer than you breathe in. The exhale is the part that applies the brake.
A sighYou already do this many times an hour. Dana’s move is simply to catch one and let it land.
Your voiceHumming, singing, reading aloud. The nerve runs through your throat, so sound reaches it directly.
MovementWalking, shaking out your hands. Imagining the movement counts, if doing it feels too exposed.
OutsideNature, Dana says, is reliably good at this — one of the few things that works for almost everyone.
A faceEyes, a familiar voice, a video call. Borrowed regulation is still regulation.

The short version

Your body decides first, and it is always trying to keep you alive.

Learn its signals, stop arguing with them, and give it the small physical things it needs to climb back up.
