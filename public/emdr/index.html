EMDR, for beginners

A memory
that never
got filed

A therapy for trauma: for when something terrible happened and it keeps coming back as if it were now. Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing, developed by psychologist Francine Shapiro.

Scroll

01  /  A memory that stays raw

Some memories never turn into the past.

Shapiro’s idea: most experiences get sorted and filed as done. Some are too much to sort. They stay exactly as they were.

All of it, kept together.

“It actually gets stored in the brain with the emotions and the physical sensations and the beliefs that were there at the time.”

Francine Shapiro, psychologist

02  /  Why it comes back

New things have to plug into old ones.

Everything that happens gets checked against what is already stored. Brush against a raw memory and it opens, feelings and all, as if it were now.

You get anxious in a meeting and have no idea it is thirty years old.

“If something happens 30 years later as an adult that is similar in any way, it has to link up with the memory networks.”

Francine Shapiro

03  /  The walk

A walk, 1987.

Shapiro noticed her own upsetting thoughts fading as she walked through a park. Her eyes had been flicking back and forth. She tried it on purpose, then on other people.

She published the first study two years later.

04  /  The model

The brain is built to file experience.

Shapiro’s picture: you have a processing system that takes what happened, keeps the useful part and lets the rest go. A bad enough experience jams it.

Get the system working again.

EMDR restarts processing for that memory. The system does the rest.

“We identify what those earlier experiences are and we process them. We bring that information processing system back online.”

Francine Shapiro

05  /  A session

Hold the picture. Follow the hand.

You bring up the worst moment of the memory: the picture, the feeling, where it sits in your body, and what it says about you. Then the therapist’s hand moves (or a light, or a pointer) and your eyes follow.

After each set of passes: what do you notice now? Then another set. You say as much or as little as you want.

“Very rapid associations and connections or insights are made, and the emotions, physical sensations, beliefs — all of those shift.”

Francine Shapiro

06  /  What changes

It becomes something that happened.

The picture fades. The feeling drops. What the memory says about you moves from then to now.

The picture

Sharp and close. Then faded and far.

The feeling

Happening now. Then over.

The belief

I am in danger. Then: that was then.

“It appears that the original memory is transformed as these connections are made.”

Francine Shapiro

07  /  The eyes

Do the eye movements matter?

Shapiro linked them to dreaming sleep, when the day gets sorted. A 2013 review combined fifteen clinical trials.

Without them,
the memory still fades.

With them,
it fades more.

A moderate difference. Taps or tones can replace the eye movements. The mechanism is still debated.

“EMDR processing seems to link in to the same processes that occur during rapid eye movement sleep.”

Francine Shapiro

08  /  The practice

Eight phases, and the eyes are only one.

The other seven phases make the work safe.

1

History

What happened, and which memories to work on.

2

Preparation

Ways to settle yourself that you can use alone.

3

Assessment

Pick one memory. The picture, the belief, the feeling, the body.

4

Desensitisation

Sets of eye movements until the memory stops hurting.

5

Installation

Attach the belief you want in its place.

6

Body scan

Think of it again. Anything left in the body?

7

Closure

Back to steady before you leave the room.

8

Re-evaluation

Next session, bring it back. How does it feel now?

Weekly sessions of up to ninety minutes, for around three months.

The short version

Bring up the memory, move your eyes, and let the brain finish the job.

If Shapiro is right, the system that files ordinary days can file the worst ones too.

EMDR is one of the most tested trauma therapies, and the argument is about why it works. The US Veterans Affairs guideline rates it “Strong For”; the American Psychological Association’s 2025 guideline gives it a conditional recommendation. A 2020 review of seventy-six trials found a large effect against no treatment, with only four of twenty-seven PTSD studies at low risk of bias. Whether the eye movements are needed is still contested. The numbers above are the researchers’, not Shapiro’s.
